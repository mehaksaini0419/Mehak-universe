The question

Half of all transactions attach an accessory. The other half don't, and each missed attach is about $72.50 of pure add-on revenue walking out of the store. So the question is simply: what separates the two halves?

The instinctive answer in retail is the customer — younger buyers take cases and chargers, older ones don't. That is testable, so I tested it first, and then kept going when it turned out to be wrong.

What I found

Three findings and one refusal to ship a number.

F1

Age band explains almost nothing

Across 5,148 transactions the four age bands attach accessories at 50.5%, 49.9%, 48.5% and 47.7%. The entire spread is 2.8 percentage points, and it runs in the intuitive direction so weakly that it would not survive a significance test on smaller samples. Average device price is flat too — $461 to $475 across all four bands.

This is the finding that stops a bad project. Had I reported the ordering without the spread, "26–40 year-olds attach most" would have become a targeting rule, and the business would have spent real money segmenting a population that behaves identically.

Recommendation

Do not build customer-level targeting on age. Report the spread, not the ranking, and redirect the analysis to where the variance actually lives.

F2

Two reps in one store are 7.9 points apart

In Store A, Jasmine attaches on 54.5% of her 486 sales. Carlos, in the same store, on the same catalogue, to the same customer base, attaches on 46.6% of his 451. Store B shows the same shape: Tina 50.9%, Marcus 47.8%. The rep-level spread across the company runs from 44.9% to 54.5% — nearly ten points, against 2.8 for age.

Sizing it: if every rep reached Jasmine's rate, the company adds 256 attaches a month. At the observed $72.50 average accessory sale that is $18,563 a month, or $222,751 a year. The conservative version — lifting only below-average reps to the company average of 49.6% — is still $49,643 a year, and that one requires no one to become exceptional.

Recommendation

Pair Carlos with Jasmine and Marcus with Tina for two weeks of side-by-side selling, and re-measure at 30 days. The intervention is free, the comparison is already controlled for store and customer mix, and the floor case pays for itself several times over. Track attach rate per rep on the weekly scorecard, not per store — the store average hides exactly the gap worth closing.

F3

Insurance is a store problem, not a rep problem

Insurance attachment splits differently. Store E reaches 44.2% and Store A 42.4%, while Store B sits at 33.9% — a 10.3-point spread, wider than the accessory gap and clustered by store rather than by individual. Store A's two reps differ by 7.9 points on accessories but sit together near the top on insurance.

That pattern points at something the store shares and the rep doesn't: how the offer is scripted at point of sale, or what the manager inspects. Home internet tells the same story from the other end — 12.5% attachment company-wide, low enough that it is a product-positioning question rather than a coaching one.

Recommendation

Treat insurance and accessories as two separate coaching programmes. Accessories are a rep-skill gap and respond to pairing; insurance is a process gap and needs the Store E and Store A scripts documented and rolled out to Store B, C and F.

F4

One query returns a number I won't report

The pace-to-goal query (Q5) is written correctly and returns nonsense: every store lands between 207% and 533% of goal. Store F comes out at 533%. No retailer beats plan by five times and calls it a plan.

The cause is a definition mismatch, not a bug. The six stores carry monthly goals totalling 1,615, while the sales table holds 5,148 rows for the same month. monthly_goal is an activation target — new lines — and sales counts every transaction, including upgrades, accessory-only tickets and add-ons. Dividing one by the other is arithmetic, not measurement.

Run Q5 in the console above and you will see it. I left the query in the repository because the SQL is right and the pattern is reusable; what I won't do is put 533% on a slide.

Recommendation

Before this metric ships, define the numerator to match the target: filter sales to new activations, or carry a separate transaction target alongside the activation goal. Until then the pace-to-goal view should be marked not-reportable rather than quietly rescaled to look sensible.

One more caveat on Q9

The revenue-quartile query ranks Sofia, Priya, Andre and Devon in the top half and the four reps from the two-rep stores in the bottom half. That ordering is mostly staffing, not skill: a sole rep in Store C handles all 854 of its sales, while Store A's volume is split between two people. Revenue per rep is not a performance measure when staffing differs by store. Attach rate is, which is why F2 uses it.

What I'd validate first with real data

  1. Whether Jasmine's advantage is technique or traffic. Same store does not guarantee same shift — a weekend rep sees a different customer than a Tuesday-morning rep. Shift-level data would settle it, and it is the one thing that could overturn F2.
  2. Whether the accessory margin holds at $72.50 across categories. The opportunity is sized on revenue; the decision should be made on margin.
  3. What Store E and Store A actually do differently on insurance. The data shows the gap and cannot show the cause.
  4. How monthly_goal is defined in the source system, which turns Q5 from broken into usable.

Method

Schema
5 normalised tables — stores, employees, customers, plans, sales — with primary and foreign keys and three indexes on the fact table
Volume
5,148 sales · 900 customers · 8 reps · 6 stores · 4 plans · November 2025
SQL
CTEs, RANK and ROW_NUMBER with PARTITION BY, LAG for day-over-day, NTILE for quartiles, unbounded-preceding running totals, conditional aggregation
Dialect
The repository is PostgreSQL. The browser console runs SQLite, so two lines differ: ::NUMERIC becomes CAST(… AS REAL) in Q5, and DATE_TRUNC('week', …) becomes date(…, 'weekday 1', '-7 days') in Q10. Everything else is identical.
Checks
Store totals reconcile to the transaction count; rep revenue sums to company revenue; every figure on this page was recomputed from the database rather than carried from an earlier report
