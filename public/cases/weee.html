A margin leak hiding behind a good quarter

The question

Every market was at or above its revenue target, so the monthly deck said the business was healthy. The question I set myself was the one a finance partner actually gets asked: is it? Revenue attainment is a gross number. It says nothing about what each order costs to fulfil, what share of revenue is being given away, or whether the customers arriving are staying.

So I built the P&L down to contribution margin by market and month, then looked for places where a cost line was moving while revenue held still — the pattern that hides inside a good top line.

What I found

Four things, three of them problems. Together they account for roughly $1.9M of annualised contribution margin that the revenue view was concealing.

F1

Los Angeles is paying 22% more per delivery than it did in January

Delivery cost per order in LA sat flat at about $6.00 for all of 2025. From January 2026 it climbs every single month — $6.30, $6.56, $6.76, $7.07, $7.35, $7.68 — while order volume and revenue hold steady. No other market moves more than 1% across the same period.

At June's volume of 75,984 orders, the gap between the current rate and January's rate is $105,070 a month, or about $1.26M annualised. None of it shows up in a revenue-versus-target view, which is why it ran for six months.

Recommendation

Audit the City of Industry fulfilment centre's routing and carrier mix against January's baseline before the next planning cycle. The step-change timing points to a contract or route change rather than gradual drift, which means it is likely reversible. Hold LA's contribution margin, not its revenue, as the reported metric.

F2

Seattle's refund rate has tripled, and it is concentrated in one category

Seattle ran at the company norm of about 1.1% of revenue refunded through September 2025. From October it climbs without pause to 4.10% by June 2026 — close to four times every other market, which all stay between 1.07% and 1.12%.

The category view says where it is coming from. Seafood & Meat carries a 21.0% gross margin in Seattle against 28–32% for every other category in that market. A rising refund rate sitting on top of the thinnest-margin category is a product quality problem, not a customer behaviour problem.

Recommendation

Run a supplier audit on Seattle's Seafood & Meat vendors, starting with anything onboarded or re-contracted around September 2025. Refunds at 4.1% on that category's volume are costing more than the margin it earns. This is a cold-chain and vendor-quality investigation, and it should be scoped as one.

F3

New York / New Jersey is buying orders it isn't keeping

NY/NJ gives away 8.5% of revenue in promotional discounts. Every other market sits at 4.5%. If the spend were working, NY/NJ would show the strongest retention. It shows the weakest: a 56.3% repeat-order rate against 66% across the other growth markets and 74% in the mature Bay Area.

Double the discount, the lowest loyalty. The promotional spend is purchasing one-time orders, and the market's cohorts are not maturing the way Bay Area's did at the same stage.

Recommendation

Move roughly half of the NY/NJ promotional budget from acquisition discounting into second- and third-order incentives, and measure it on 90-day repeat rate instead of order count. Bay Area's curve is the benchmark to aim the market at — it reached 74% repeat on 4.5% promotional spend.

F4

The targets are being set from the top down, and it shows

Attainment is not stable — it oscillates and then decays. January and February of both years overshoot hard (113.6%, 125.5%, then 115.2%, 125.2%) because the targets do not carry Lunar New Year seasonality, which lifts Chinese, Korean and Vietnamese categories by roughly a fifth. Every other month lands within a couple of points of 100%, and the last three months of the window come in at 100.9%, 100.3%, 99.6% and 100.2%.

A plan that misses two months by twenty-five points and hits the rest almost exactly is not a forecast. It is an annual number divided by twelve, with the seasonality left out.

Recommendation

Rebuild next year's plan bottom-up by market, with a seasonality index applied at category level so Q1 carries the Lunar New Year lift explicitly. The current method makes January look like outperformance and makes June look like a miss, when neither is true.

What's working

Houston is the one to fund. Orders grew 41% over the window, from 21,543 a month to 30,338, on the lowest delivery cost per order in the network at $5.84 and promotional spend at the 4.5% norm. It is the newest market, opened in 2022, and it is growing without being bought. Sacramento is the counterpart — steady, cheap to serve, but only 42,892 new customers against Houston's 136,530, which suggests it is under-invested rather than underperforming.

What I'd validate first with real data

  1. Whether LA's cost step-change is a carrier contract, a route-density change, or a zone-mix shift — the dataset shows the effect, not the cause, and the recommendation changes depending on which it is.
  2. Whether Seattle's refunds are concentrated in specific SKUs or specific vendors. Category-level is as deep as this data goes; a vendor audit needs SKU and lot detail.
  3. Whether NY/NJ's promotional spend is acquisition or reactivation. Those look identical in a discount total and call for opposite responses.
  4. How the targets were actually built. I inferred top-down allocation from the residual pattern, which is strong evidence but not proof.
